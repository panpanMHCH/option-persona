For an out-of-the-money option both price and delta depend mainly on total volatility sigma*sqrt(T), so price and delta together cannot separate sigma from T. The ~7-day inference rests on the premium *and* the recorded 46% IV; delta -0.29 is consistent with it (model: -0.288) but adds almost no independent information. Only the recorded entry timestamp can settle it.

Across S = 201–202 the premium-implied horizon spans Fri 30 Oct 05:40 to Sat 31 Oct 04:14 ET (calendar time), which points to the **Friday 30 October 2026 session** as the most plausible entry. This is an inference, not a recorded timestamp: calendar-day versus trading-day time conventions alone move it by about a day, because a weekend sits inside the window.

## What this case teaches

1. **Defined risk is exact; everything else is a model.** The $238 maximum loss and the $192.62 breakeven need no model at all. Every pre-expiry number depends on σ, which the market changes constantly.

2. **Your recorded numbers are internally consistent, and that's informative.** Given S ≈ 201.5 and 46% IV, a $2.38 premium implies about 7 calendar days to expiry, so you bought roughly a one-week option.

   Delta adds almost no independent information, because both price and delta of an OTM option depend mainly on total volatility σ√T. To audit a trade later you need the timestamp and the quote; that's why the journal (Phase 3) records provenance.

3. **Leverage, quantified.** The $239 put carried about −29 share-equivalents (≈ $5,800 of short exposure) with elasticity ≈ −24. A 1% NVDA move changed the option's value by roughly 24%. That's 24× magnification in *both* directions: a +5% NVDA rally costs about 75% of the premium in the instantaneous scenario.

4. **Gamma and theta are two sides of one trade.** One week from expiry, the position lost about $30/day at an unchanged spot, and the decay accelerates: $239 → $139 → $60 → $0 over the final week.

   In exchange, delta steepens rapidly as NVDA falls (−0.29 → −0.53 after a 4% drop, at 7 days). That's why the option's value "changed substantially" when NVDA declined sharply.

   Whether a decline makes money depends on **how fast** it happens. The same −4% move is +$329 with 7 days left but only +$35 with 1 day left.

5. **Volatility moves with price.** Sharp equity declines usually lift implied volatility. Market prices during a sell-off would likely have exceeded the constant-46% model values above, a vega gain on top of the delta and gamma gains. The spot × vol stress grid in the dashboard shows this.

6. **Real-world versus risk-neutral.** The model's N(−d₂) gives the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money. It says nothing about whether NVDA was *likely* to fall. A put's expected real-world return is typically negative before costs (long a volatility risk premium, short an equity risk premium), so the decision rests on your own view and sizing, not on the model.
